### CHINA — BEIJING

#### Museum/Site Strategy (1 per day, with hidden corners)

**Day 2: Forbidden City (Palace Museum)**
- **Best entry time**: 8:30 AM at Meridian Gate (before tour buses arrive at 9:30)
- **Hidden corners**: Treasure Gallery (珍宝馆) in the northeast — less visited, contains finest imperial jewelry; Clock Gallery (钟表馆) in the west — magnificent European clocks gifted by Jesuits
- **Booking**: Tickets MUST be booked online in advance at pm.com.cn; timed entry; no walk-up sales
- **Crowd avoidance**: Inner courts (Yonghe Gong area) are far less visited than outer ceremonial halls
- **Duration**: 3 hours minimum; 5 hours for deep exploration

**Day 3: Great Wall (Mutianyu)**
- **Cultural layer**: The wall was built by 1 million conscripted laborers over centuries; each tower has a unique history; watchtower inscriptions still visible
- **Best time**: First cable car up at 8 AM; quieter before 10 AM
- **Hidden corner**: Walk northeast past Tower 14 to the unrestored section — you're truly alone with history

**Day 4: Temple of Heaven**
- **Best time**: 8–9 AM to see elderly Beijingers doing tai chi, playing erhu, and dancing in the park — this is the real show, not just the buildings
- **Hidden corner**: Echo Wall (circular wall around the Imperial Vault of Heaven) — whisper travels along the curve; most tourists miss the correct technique
- **Booking**: Online at tiantanpark.com; also sold at gate

**Day 5: Summer Palace**
- **Best time**: 9 AM weekday; less crowded than weekends
- **Hidden corner**: The Garden of Harmonious Interests (谐趣园) in the northeast corner — a private imperial garden within the garden; locals call it the "Garden of Suzhou inside Beijing"
- **Booking**: Online preferred; also at gate (but queues form quickly)

#### Alternative Culture — Beijing
- **Live performance**: Peking Opera (京剧) at Lao She Teahouse — attend an evening teahouse performance; 1–1.5 hrs; condensed highlights, English surtitles; book online ~$30/person
- **Spiritual site**: White Cloud Temple (白云观) in Xuanwu — active Taoist monastery; far fewer tourists than Buddhist sites; free entry; monk processions visible

#### Deep History — 3 Surprising Facts
1. The Forbidden City's moat is 6 meters deep — constructed as a defensive system, the soil excavated was used to build the artificial Coal Hill (Jingshan) to the north
2. Beijing has three times the number of hutong alleyways that remain from the Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368) as Venice has canals — over 1,000 hutongs still exist
3. The Summer Palace's Long Corridor (728 meters) contains 14,000 individual paintings — no two are the same, making it the world's longest painted gallery

#### Cultural Transitions — China → Vietnam
- **Greeting shift**: Chinese nod/slight bow at formal settings → Vietnamese bow or two-hand handshake
- **Dining shift**: Chinese prefer hot tea; Vietnamese prefer iced coffee and iced tea
- **Pace shift**: Beijing is a metropolitan capital, fast-paced; Hanoi and Hội An are slower, more meandering
- **Religion shift**: Beijing is primarily secular/Taoist/Buddhist; Vietnam is more actively Buddhist with visible temple practices
- **"Culture shock" buffer**: The China → Vietnam transition is significant but positive — Vietnam feels warmer (temperature and people)

#### Language Reality — China
- English penetration in Beijing: **Low outside luxury hotels and tourist sites** — staff at 5-star hotels are fluent; subway signage is bilingual; beyond that, assume no English
- Survival phrases:
  - 你好 (Nǐ hǎo) — Hello
  - 谢谢 (Xièxiè) — Thank you
  - 多少钱 (Duōshǎo qián) — How much?
  - 我不要 (Wǒ bùyào) — I don't want it (essential for market hawkers)
  - 帮我叫滴滴 (Bāng wǒ jiào Dīdī) — Please call me a DiDi (for hotel staff)
  - 厕所在哪里 (Cèsuǒ zài nǎlǐ) — Where is the toilet?
  - 太贵了 (Tài guì le) — Too expensive (for market bargaining)
- **Translation app**: Google Translate camera mode (Chinese Simplified pack downloaded) — point at menu, instant translation

### VIETNAM — HANOI

#### Museum/Site Strategy

**Day 7: Ngoc Son Temple + Temple of Literature**
- **Ngoc Son Temple**: Open 8 AM; cross the Huc Bridge (red lacquer arch bridge) over Hoan Kiem Lake; small, peaceful, not crowded; 30 min max
- **Temple of Literature**: Best at 9 AM–11 AM on weekdays; afternoon more crowded; the second and third courtyards are the most photogenic; doctoral stone steles with names of exam graduates carved in 1484 — you can find 500-year-old names
- **Hidden corner**: The Khue Van Cac Pavilion (Pavilion of Constellation of Literature) in the third courtyard — rarely noticed but the most iconic architectural element

**Day 8: Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum**
- **Open**: Closed Mon and Fri; open Tue/Wed/Thu/Sat/Sun 7:30–10:30 AM only — Day 8 is a Tuesday ✓
- **Protocol**: Dress code strictly enforced (covered shoulders and knees); no photography inside; queue moves quickly; 15–20 min total
- **Adjacent**: Ho Chi Minh Museum and stilt house — can skip museum but the stilt house garden is worth 15 min

#### Alternative Culture — Hanoi
- **Live performance**: Water Puppet Theatre (Thăng Long Water Puppet Theatre) — uniquely Vietnamese art form; 50 min show; book online 1–2 days ahead; ~$5–8/ticket
- **Neighborhood walk**: Tay Ho (West Lake) neighborhood — expat area, beautiful lakeside temples (Tran Quoc Pagoda on an island), far fewer tourists than Old Quarter

#### Deep History — Hanoi
1. Hoan Kiem Lake's name means "Lake of the Returned Sword" — a legend where Emperor Le Loi returned a magical sword to a giant golden turtle in the lake; a giant softshell turtle actually lived in the lake until 2016 (died at ~200 years old)
2. Hanoi's Old Quarter's 36 streets were each assigned a single trade in the 15th century — Paper Street, Tin Street, Silk Street — many retain the same trade 600 years later
3. The Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum was built against Ho Chi Minh's explicit wishes; he wanted to be cremated and have his ashes scattered across three regions of Vietnam

### VIETNAM — HỘI AN

#### Museum/Site Strategy

**Day 10: Hội An Ancient Town**
- **Best entry time**: 8 AM before tour buses; or return after 5 PM for lantern atmosphere
- **UNESCO ticket**: Required to enter historic houses and assembly halls; ~120,000 VND; covers 5 sites of your choice; available at entrance gates
- **Hidden corner**: Phung Hung Old House (interior courtyard; family has lived here 8 generations); Tan Ky Old House (Japanese-Chinese architecture fusion; the family still lives here)
- **Museum fatigue check**: ⚠️ Don't try to hit all 5 sites on one visit — 2–3 is the sweet spot for the relaxed pace profile

**Day 12: My Son Sanctuary**
- **Best time**: 7:30 AM opening — crucial to beat both heat and tour groups
- **Hidden corner**: Groups B, C, D (the ones further from the main path) — better preserved, almost no visitors
- **Note**: Allow 1.5–2 hrs total; hire a local guide at gate (~$5–10) for context that transforms ruins into history

#### Alternative Culture — Hội An
- **Traditional craft**: Lantern-making class at one of the Old Quarter workshops (~$10/person; 1 hr; take home your lantern)
- **Morning cooking class**: Morning Glory Cooking School — see [[06-food-dining]] for details
- **Spiritual site**: Phuc Kien Assembly Hall (built by Fujian Chinese immigrants in 1697) — ornate Taoist-Buddhist hybrid; active place of worship; respectful visit welcome

#### Deep History — Hội An
1. Hội An was the most important trading port in Southeast Asia from the 15th–19th centuries, visited by Japanese, Chinese, Dutch, Portuguese, and Arab merchants — explaining the architectural fusion unique to the town
2. The Japanese Covered Bridge (Chùa Cầu) was built circa 1593 by the Japanese merchant community to connect with the Chinese quarter — it has a small temple inside the bridge itself dedicated to a deity who controls earthquakes
3. During the Vietnam War, Hội An was almost entirely spared from bombing — both sides considered it culturally significant — which is why it's the only major Vietnamese city that survived essentially intact

#### Language Reality — Vietnam
- English penetration: **High in tourist zones** (Hội An Ancient Town, Old Quarter Hanoi, Da Nang resorts); reasonable in hotels; lower in rural/market settings
- Survival phrases:
  - Xin chào (Sin Chow) — Hello
  - Cảm ơn (Kahm Uhn) — Thank you
  - Bao nhiêu tiền? (Bow Nyew Tyen?) — How much?
  - Không (Khong) — No
  - Có WiFi không? (Koh WiFi Khong?) — Is there WiFi?
  - Tôi muốn... (Toy Mwun) — I want...
  - Gọi cho tôi Grab (Goy Cho Toy Grab) — Call me a Grab
- **Translation app**: Google Translate works normally in Vietnam; Vietnamese pack downloaded
