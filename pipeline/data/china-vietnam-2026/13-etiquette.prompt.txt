### CHINA — BEIJING

#### 10 Behavioral Norms

1. **Don't tip** — Tipping is not customary and can embarrass service staff; exception: tour guides and porters (¥20–50/day acceptable and appreciated for guides); 5-star hotel staff follow international norms and won't refuse tips
2. **Greet with a nod or slight bow** — Handshakes are acceptable at business settings; a respectful nod is sufficient for everyday interactions; no hugging strangers
3. **Two hands for business cards/gifts** — Present and receive with both hands; this is a mark of respect; inspect what you receive before putting it away
4. **Be direct about food/drink preferences** — Chinese hosts take pride in hospitality through food; politely declining requires firmness: "我吃饱了" (Wǒ chī bǎo le = I'm full); declining multiple times is expected before acceptance
5. **No photos inside government buildings and many temples** — Look for 禁止拍照 (no photography) signs; always ask at temples; the Forbidden City outer areas are fine, inner galleries sometimes not
6. **Dress modestly at temples** — Shoulders and knees covered at Buddhist/Taoist temples; the White Cloud Temple (Taoist) is stricter than tourist-oriented Buddhist temples
7. **Avoid public displays of affection in conservative areas** — Fine in Sanlitun or near 5-star hotels; less appropriate near temples or older neighborhoods
8. **Don't point with your finger** — Use an open hand to gesture; pointing at people with index finger is rude
9. **Bargaining is expected in markets but not in shops/malls** — Silk Street and Wangfujing markets: bargain (start at 20–30% of asking price); any mall or restaurant: fixed price
10. **Loudness in restaurants is normal** — Noise is not rudeness in Chinese restaurant culture; don't be surprised or alarmed; it's a sign of a lively meal

#### 5 Conversation Starters — China
1. "这是我第一次来北京，你最喜欢哪个地方？" (This is my first time in Beijing — what's your favorite place?) — locals love sharing recommendations
2. Ask about their job or their family (indirect route — "Do you have children?" is not rude in China)
3. Comment on the food: "真好吃！" (Zhēn hào chī = Really delicious!) — universal icebreaker
4. Ask about the history of a place: "这里有多少年历史了？" (How old is this place?) — sparks pride
5. Inquire about local events: "最近有什么好玩的活动吗？" (Anything fun happening locally?) — locals love revealing off-the-beaten-path events

#### Gestures & Taboos — China
- **Avoid**: Clock as a gift (钟 zhōng sounds like 终 zhōng = "end/death"); green hats (associated with infidelity)
- **Avoid**: Sticking chopsticks upright in rice (resembles incense sticks at funeral offerings)
- **Avoid**: Talking about Taiwan as a country, Tibet independence — never raise in conversation
- **Thumbs up**: Acceptable; means good/great
- **OK sign** (circle with thumb and index): Acceptable
- **Waving "no"**: Shake hand side to side (not index finger wagging like in West)

#### Supporting Locals — China
- Shop at independent hutong artisan shops over tourist market stalls (better quality, more of the money stays local)
- Eat at locally-recommended spots rather than tourist restaurants near Tiananmen
- Use DiDi over ride-hailing services that exploit drivers; tip your local guide

### VIETNAM — ALL CITIES

#### 10 Behavioral Norms

1. **Tip, but softly** — 10–15% at restaurants with table service; round up at street stalls; tip tour guides and drivers ($1–2/person/day); nothing is expected but everything is appreciated
2. **Remove shoes at temple entrances** — Always look for a pile of shoes; follow the lead of locals entering
3. **Dress modestly at temples and pagodas** — Sarongs often available to borrow at temple entrances if you forget; tank tops alone are not sufficient
4. **Don't touch the heads of adults or children** — In Vietnamese (and broadly Buddhist) culture, the head is sacred
5. **Haggling at markets** — Appropriate at Ben Thanh, Dong Xuan, and any open market; smile while negotiating; it's friendly sport
6. **Receiving with two hands** — Same as China for gifts and formal items
7. **Eldest person is served first, seated first** — If at a Vietnamese family's table, observe who sits before sitting
8. **The Vietnamese V-for-victory pose in photos** — Locals (especially young women) make the V sign in virtually every photo; it's not a political gesture — join in if invited
9. **Motorbike traffic crossing** — Walk slowly and steadily across the street; motorbikes will flow around you; DO NOT freeze or rush; consistent pace is the key
10. **Do not raise your voice or show frustration publicly** — "Face" (thể diện) is important; public confrontation shames both parties; always resolve disputes quietly

#### 5 Conversation Starters — Vietnam
1. Ask about their hometown — Vietnamese are very proud of their region of origin; "Anh/Chị quê ở đâu?" (Where are you from originally?) opens many doors
2. Comment genuinely on Vietnamese food: "Tôi rất thích ăn phở" (I really like pho) — instant warmth
3. Ask about the war (carefully): Vietnamese people process the war differently than Americans might expect — for younger generations, it's history, not trauma; "My parents watched the war news as children — what do people here know about that time?" is a respectful opener
4. Ask about their family: family is the center of Vietnamese life; photos of children are an instant connection
5. Express curiosity about a craft: "Cái này được làm như thế nào?" (How is this made?) at any artisan shop

#### Gestures & Taboos — Vietnam
- **Avoid**: Pointing with index finger (use open hand); crossing fingers in photos (symbolizes the female genitalia in Vietnam — locals will react); showing the bottom of your feet to someone (insulting)
- **Avoid**: Snapping fingers to get a server's attention (rude); instead, make eye contact and give a slight nod
- **OK sign**: Acceptable
- **Thumbs up**: Acceptable and common
- **Wai (hands clasped in prayer)**: Acceptable greeting at temples; not necessary in everyday settings

#### Supporting Locals — Vietnam
- Stay at locally-owned boutique hotels in Hội An (not just the Đà Nẵng mega-resorts) for one night if possible
- Buy directly from artisans at Kim Bong and Thanh Ha villages (see [[12-hidden-gems]])
- Order Vietnamese coffee at local shops, not Highlands Coffee (chain)
- Tip your Grab drivers — they're gig workers earning very little per ride

### Cross-Cultural Transition Guide: China → Vietnam

| Aspect | China (Beijing) | Vietnam (Hanoi/Hội An) |
|--------|----------------|----------------------|
| Greeting | Nod, no touch | Friendly, may attempt handshake |
| Tipping | Mostly no | Yes, appreciated |
| Pace | Efficient, direct | Relaxed, meandering |
| Eye contact | Moderate | Friendly, more direct |
| English | Very limited | Moderate in tourist areas |
| Noise level | Louder (normal) | Moderate |
| Bargaining | Aggressive (markets) | Gentler, playful |
| Food pace | Fast | Slow and social |

**"Cultural whiplash" buffer**: The shift is positive for most Western travelers — Vietnam feels warmer, more outwardly expressive, and more English-friendly. Don't over-adjust; maintain the same respectful curiosity.

**Key mindset shift at China→Vietnam border**: In Beijing, efficiency wins; in Vietnam, relationships win. Slow down, smile more, linger over meals. The Vietnamese word "tà tà" (slowly slowly) captures the ethos.
